Scratch tutorial · 8 steps · about 3–4 hours

Build Dust Hopper

Dust Hopper is a landing game. You fly a small survey drone over an asteroid and set it down gently on a landing pad. Along the way you will learn how real game physics work: speed, gravity, thrust and angles.

You need the free Scratch editor at scratch.mit.edu/projects/editor. Each step ends with a checkpoint file, so you can compare your project or catch up.

The plan

  1. Set the sceneBackdrop, ground, pads and drone in place
  2. GravityThe drone falls faster and faster, and stops on the ground
  3. EngineThrottle with Up and Down, thrust against gravity
  4. TiltingRotate, and split thrust into sideways and upward parts
  5. Screen edgesFly off one side and come back on the other
  6. LandingPerfect landing, hard landing or crash
  7. Fuel and scoreBurn fuel, earn points, game over
  8. PolishEngine flame, boost key and instructions
1

Set the scene

A game needs a world before it needs rules. In this step you load the artwork and make sure every sprite starts in the same place each time you click the green flag.

Get the artwork

  1. Download the starter project. It has the artwork but no code.
  2. In the Scratch editor, choose File › Load from your computer and pick starter.sb3.
  3. Look around. There is a backdrop called space and three sprites: Ground (the rocks), Pads (the yellow landing pads) and Drone, which has four costumes: drone, drone-flame, drone-boost and boom.

Starter project: artwork only, no code.

Download starter.sb3

Drawing your own? Keep the ground and the pads as two separate sprites, each as big as the whole Stage. Later the drone asks “am I touching the Pads?” and “am I touching the Ground?”, and those two questions need two sprites. Draw the drone about 40 pixels wide, with its feet at the bottom.

Where things are on the Stage

The Stage is 480 pixels wide and 360 tall. The centre is x: 0, y: 0. x grows to the right, up to 240. y grows upwards, up to 180. The drone starts at x: -60, y: 150: a little left of centre, near the top.

Click each sprite in the sprite list and drag in these blocks. The go to blocks put things back where they belong, even if you dragged a sprite by accident. point in direction 90 means “stand up straight”: in Scratch, direction 90 shows a costume exactly as it was drawn.

2

Gravity

Things do not fall at a steady speed. They start slowly and get faster. Games copy this with two simple rules, repeated in every frame (Scratch draws about 30 frames each second):

  1. Gravity changes the speed: the falling speed grows a little.
  2. Speed changes the position: the drone moves by its current speed.

Because the speed keeps growing, each move is a bit bigger than the last. Here are the numbers from the code below, frame by frame:

FrameTimeSpeed vyHeight posYDescent display
start0 s01500
1-0.035149.9650
2-0.07149.8951
3-0.105149.791

903 s-3.156.732

In the first second the drone drops about 16 pixels. In the third second it drops almost 80. That is gravity.

Make the variables

  1. Go to the Variables category and click Make a Variable. Create gravity, posX, posY, vy and Descent, each For all sprites.
  2. Untick the checkbox next to every variable except Descent, so only Descent shows on the Stage.

Why posX and posY? Scratch already knows where the sprite is, but it will not let a sprite leave the Stage. Keeping the position in our own variables means the numbers are always exactly what our code says, and in step 5 it lets the drone fly off one edge and come back on the other.

Add the code

Replace the Drone’s script from step 1 with this one. Leave the Ground and Pads scripts as they are.

Drone

How it works

  • The four set blocks reset everything, so each run starts the same way.
  • repeat until runs its inside once per frame, until the drone touches the rocks or a pad.
  • change vy by (0 − gravity) makes the speed more negative each frame. Negative speed means moving down. Scratch has no “minus” block for a variable, so we subtract it from 0.
  • change posY by vy moves the drone by its speed, then go to shows it at the new place.
  • Descent turns the speed into a friendly number: it is positive while falling and ten times the speed, rounded. Later, Descent decides whether a landing is gentle or a crash.

Check it

Click the green flag. The drone falls, gets faster, and stops on the rocks with a message. With the starting values it lands at a Descent of about 38.

Try this

  • Set gravity to 0.01. The drone floats down slowly, like on a small moon. What Descent do you get?
  • Set gravity to 0.1. How much faster is the touchdown?
  • Set the starting posX to -115 (in both places). The drone now lands on the ×2 pad.

3

Engine

Now the drone gets an engine. The engine pushes upwards with a force, and gravity still pulls down. Whichever is bigger wins. You control the force with a throttle from 0 (off) to 15 (full power).

Make the variables

  1. Create Throttle, force and cooldown, each For all sprites.
  2. Keep Throttle ticked so it shows on the Stage. Untick the other two.

How strong is the engine?

The force is Throttle × 0.004. Gravity is 0.035, so the engine balances gravity at a throttle of about 9:

Throttleforceforce − gravityWhat happens
00-0.035Falls, like step 2
80.032-0.003Sinks very slowly
90.036+0.001Rises very slowly
150.060+0.025Climbs

Add the code

Here is the whole Drone script after this step. The new parts are the two set blocks at the start for Throttle and cooldown, the big if … else for the keys, the set force block, and the gravity line, which now reads change vy by (force − gravity).

Drone

How it works

  • The key checks raise or lower the throttle by one step. The loop runs about 30 times a second, so holding a key would race to 15 almost at once.
  • cooldown fixes that. After each step it is set to 3, and the keys are only read again once it has counted back down to 0. That gives about 10 steps per second while a key is held.
  • change vy by (force − gravity) is the same gravity rule as before, with the engine pushing back.

Flying too high? If you climb for a long time the drone sticks at the top edge of the Stage. Step 5 fixes this.

Check it

Click the green flag, then hold the Up arrow. The fall slows down. Find the throttle where the drone almost hovers.

Try this

  • Change 0.004 to 0.008. The engine is twice as strong. At what throttle does it hover now?
  • Land with a Descent below 10. That will count as a perfect landing in step 6.

4

Tilting

A real lander steers by tilting. When it leans, its engine pushes partly sideways. In this step the drone also arrives with some sideways speed, just like in the 1979 arcade game, so you have to tilt to stop it drifting.

Make the variables

  1. Create vx, angle and Drift, each For all sprites.
  2. Keep Drift ticked; untick the other two. Drift shows the sideways speed the same way Descent shows the falling speed.

Splitting the push

The engine pushes along the drone’s body. When the drone leans, that push splits into a sideways part and an upward part. Sine and cosine tell us how big each part is:

anglesin (sideways share)cos (upward share)
0° (upright)01
11.25° (one tap)0.1950.981
22.5° (two taps)0.3830.924
45°0.7070.707
90° (on its side)10

One tap of an arrow key turns the drone 11.25°, so 32 taps make a full circle, the same as the 1979 game.

Add the code

New parts: set vx to 1.2 at the start (the drift), the Right and Left arrow checks, the angle blocks, the two change vx / change vy blocks using sin and cos, change posX by vx, and the Drift display.

Drone

How it works

  • angle is the tilt away from upright. Scratch’s direction 90 means upright, so angle = direction − 90. Tilted right gives a positive angle, tilted left a negative one.
  • The if after it fixes one corner case. Scratch directions run from −180 to 180, so a drone pointing down and to the left would give an angle below −180. Adding 360 turns it into the same tilt measured the other way.
  • sin and cos are in the ( [abs v] of () ) block in Operators: choose sin or cos from its menu.

Check it

Click the green flag. The drone drifts to the right. Tap Left once or twice and add throttle: the drift slows down. Turn upright again before you land.

Screen edges

Try this with your step 4 project: let the drone drift all the way to the right. It stops at the edge and stays there, even though posX keeps growing. Scratch never lets a sprite leave the Stage; it always keeps a small part of it visible. Hiding the sprite does not help either, because hidden sprites are held back too.

Our own posX and posY have no such limit, so we decide what happens at the edges ourselves:

Add the code

New parts: three if blocks just before go to x: (posX) y: (posY).

Drone

How it works

  • Past 230 on the right, subtract 460 and the drone comes back at −230 on the left. The same in the other direction.
  • We wrap at 230 instead of 240 so the drone is never pushed against the edge, where Scratch would hold it back.
  • At the top, posY stops at 165 and any upward speed is removed, so the drone cannot climb out of sight.

Check it

Let the drone drift off the right edge. It should appear on the left side and keep moving. Climb with full throttle: it stops just below the top.

Landing

Until now, every touchdown ends the same way. Now the game judges your landing, using the same kind of rules as the 1979 game:

When the drone touches down…Result
on the rocks, not on a padCrash
tilted more than one tap (11.25°)Crash
with Drift above 10 or below −10Crash
with Descent below 10Perfect landing
with Descent 10 to 17Hard landing
with Descent 18 or moreCrash

Lists for the pads

The game needs to know where each pad is and what it is worth. Three lists hold that, one row per pad:

PadpadLeftpadRightpadMult
×2 (wide, low)-150-802
×5 (narrow, between peaks)36685
×3 (right)1401853
  1. In Variables, click Make a List and create padLeft, padRight and padMult, each For all sprites. Untick them so they don’t cover the Stage.
  2. Create the variables state, mult and i, and untick them too.

Add the code

New parts: the list blocks at the very start; set state to flying, switch costume to drone and show; the loop now repeats until state is no longer “flying”; the touching checks with the landing rules at the end of the loop; and the three result messages after the loop.

Drone

How it works

  • state remembers what is happening: flying, good, hard or crash. The loop keeps going while it says flying.
  • Touching the Pads starts the landing check. The repeat walks through the three pads with i = 1, 2, 3 and checks whether the drone is between that pad’s left and right edge. If so, mult becomes the pad’s value. If no pad matches, mult stays 0, which means a crash.
  • abs means “size without the sign”: abs of −11.25 is 11.25. It lets one check cover tilting left and right, and drifting left and right.
  • Touching the Ground without touching a pad is always a crash, and the drone switches to its boom costume.

Check it

Fly to a pad, turn upright, and come down slowly. Watch Descent as you approach.

Try this

  • Land on the ×5 pad. It is narrow and sits between two peaks.
  • Make the game easier: allow a Descent up to 14 for a perfect landing.

7

Fuel and score

Now it becomes a game with rounds. You start with 1000 fuel. Every landing earns points, and every round costs fuel. When the fuel runs out, the game is over.

Rules

ResultScoreFuel
Perfect landing+50 × pad value+50 bonus
Hard landing+15 × pad value
Crash−150
Engine running−25 × force, every frame (full throttle: −1.5)

Make the variables and a message

  1. Create Score, Fuel and fuelLeft, each For all sprites. Keep Score and Fuel ticked; untick fuelLeft.
  2. Drag a broadcast block from Events, open its menu, choose New message and name it new round.

Why two fuel variables? Fuel burns in small pieces like 0.36 per frame. fuelLeft keeps the exact amount, and Fuel shows it rounded, so the display stays readable.

Add the code

The Drone now has two scripts. when flag clicked sets up the game and starts the first round. when I receive new round is the old script: drag the blocks from set gravity down to the end under it. New parts are the fuel blocks after set force, the score and fuel changes in the result messages, and the end of the script, which either starts the next round or ends the game.

Drone

How it works

  • broadcast new round sends a message, and the when I receive script starts. At the end of a round it sends the message again, so the rounds repeat until the fuel is gone.
  • No fuel, no force: when fuelLeft drops below 0.01, force is set to 0 and the engine stops pushing.
  • stop this script ends the game but leaves the final score on the screen.

8

Polish

The game works. This last step makes it feel finished: an engine flame you can see, a boost key for emergencies, and instructions at the start.

Add the code

New parts: the instruction blocks in when flag clicked; the if key space pressed boost after set force; and in the loop, switch costume to drone before the touching checks and the flame costumes after them.

Drone

How it works

  • Boost sets the force to 0.07, more than full throttle. Because fuel burn depends on force, boosting also burns fuel faster without any extra blocks.
  • The flame trick: the flame is part of the costume, and Scratch counts every visible pixel when it checks touching. A long flame could touch the rocks before the legs do. So the drone switches to its plain costume, checks for touching, and only then puts the flame back. Scratch draws the screen once at the end of each loop, so you never see the switch.
  • The small flame shows for gentle thrust (force below 0.04), the big flame for strong thrust.

Tidy the displays

Drag the displays on the Stage so Score, Fuel and Throttle sit in the top left corner, and Drift and Descent in the top right. The drone starts in the open sky between them.

Going further

Your engine is simple: double the throttle and you double the push. The 1979 arcade game used a table of 16 values instead, so the first few throttle steps add a lot of push and the last few add only a little: 0, 2, 5, 8, 11, 13, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 22, 24, 26, 28. The full version of Dust Hopper uses that table, stored in a list. You can open it and compare.

Dust Hopper, full version, with the 1979 thrust table.

More ideas to try on your own:

  • Add sounds: an engine hum while the throttle is above 0, and a bang for a crash.
  • Make a second level: draw new rocks and pads, and change the three lists to match.
  • Add wind: a small change to vx every frame.

The physics in this game follow the 1979 arcade lander, whose original source code has been published. The game, its name, artwork and level are new.
